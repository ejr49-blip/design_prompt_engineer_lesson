Bauhaus

Founded in 1919 in Germany, the Bauhaus school unified art, craft, and industrial production under a modernist philosophy. Bauhaus graphic design emphasized functionalism, simplified geometry, and sans-serif typography. Visual elements were stripped of ornament to prioritize clarity and purpose. Rather than treating design as decoration, Bauhaus framed it as a system for solving visual problems. These principles shaped later modernist movements and influenced the development of structured graphic design systems.

Tags: modernism, functionalism, sans-serif typography, visual hierarchy, rational design

Examples: Bauhaus Archive, MoMA Collection

Not to be confused with: Mid-century modern, which emerged later and emphasized commercial styling rather than educational reform.

Verification: I verified this by checking the Bauhaus Archive and the Museum of Modern Art.

Swiss Style

Swiss Style emerged in Switzerland during the 1950s as a system for clear, objective visual communication. Designers used grid systems, asymmetrical layouts, and sans-serif typography to organize information logically. The movement reduced personal expression in favor of neutrality and consistency. Swiss Style influenced corporate and institutional design by promoting legibility, structure, and repeatable layout systems.

Tags: grid system, objectivity, asymmetrical layout, modular design, legibility

Examples: MoMA Overview, Swiss Design Awards

Not to be confused with: Minimalism, which is an aesthetic tendency rather than a communication system.

Verification: I verified this by checking MoMA’s International Typographic Style collection.

[New Wave Typography OR Grunge Typography]

New Wave Typography developed in the late 1970s and 1980s as a reaction against the strict order of Swiss Style. Designers disrupted grid systems and treated typography as expressive visual material. Legibility was sometimes reduced to challenge assumptions about neutral communication. New Wave reflected postmodern skepticism toward rigid systems and emphasized interpretation over uniform clarity.

Tags: postmodernism, experimental typography, deconstruction, typographic expression, visual disruption

Examples: AIGA Resource, Design Museum

Not to be confused with: Grunge design, which emphasizes texture and decay rather than typographic experimentation.

Verification: I verified this by checking AIGA design history resources.
